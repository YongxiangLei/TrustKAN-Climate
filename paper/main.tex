\title{Two Silent Failure Modes in Trustworthy Forecasting: An Effective-Receptive-Field Audit and Calibration-Selected Reliability Fusion}

\author{Yongxiang Lei,~\IEEEmembership{Member,~IEEE}%
\thanks{Author affiliations, funding acknowledgements, and correspondence details will be finalized before submission.}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
A forecasting model can satisfy every accuracy test a reviewer applies while silently ignoring almost all of its input, and a reliability score assembled from several signals can rank forecasts worse than one of its own inputs does alone. Both failures are invisible to the measurements usually reported, and we encountered both in a system we built ourselves. This paper contributes the two diagnostics that expose them and the corrected design they produced. The first is an \emph{effective-receptive-field audit}: a training-free perturbation sweep that reports how many input steps can actually influence a forecast. Applied to a temporal Kolmogorov--Arnold Network (KAN) configured with \robustHistory{} days of history, it finds that only \robustFieldPublished{} steps reach the readout, so a year of stated context was never read; the audit is architecture-agnostic and we recommend it wherever a contribution is stated in terms of temporal context. The second is \emph{calibration-selected fusion}: choosing the weight of a multi-signal reliability score on the calibration partition over a grid whose endpoints are the single-signal scores, which removes by construction the failure mode in which fusion degrades its own best input, and which lets the data withhold mass from a signal that does not earn it. Rebuilding the architecture around the audit --- a causal dilated stem whose reach provably spans the window, plus an attention readout --- we evaluate on \cetSpanYears{} years of daily Central England Temperature observations at horizons of 1, 7, 30, and 90 days, with five seeds, eleven comparators, and a pre-registered protocol bound to artifact checksums. Accuracy reaches parity rather than victory, and we state it as such: against the strongest baseline the corrected model wins \pairedTransformerWins{} of \pairedTransformerTotal{} seed-matched pairs, loses \pairedTransformerLosses{}, and is inconclusive on \pairedTransformerTied{} after Holm correction. It is significantly better than a budget-matched plain KAN in \pairedKanWins{} of \pairedKanTotal{} pairs, better than the published architecture in \pairedPublishedWins{} of \pairedPublishedTotal{} (up to \pairedPublishedNinety{}\,\degC{} at ninety days), and better than a wide-stem-only variant only at \pairedDilatedWinHorizons{}, which isolates the readout to the long leads the mechanism predicts. The reliability results are where the corrected design pays. Error-detection AUROC rises from \errorAurocOne{} at one day to \errorAurocNinety{} at ninety; simultaneous joint coverage holds at \simulCoverageThirty{}--\simulCoverageNinety{} against a nominal \nominalCoverage{}; and the fitted fusion weight places \fusionWeightLow{}--\fusionWeightHigh{} of the mass on interval width in every one of \fusionRuns{} runs, never the equal weighting that had been frozen in advance. We state the boundaries as plainly as the gains: day-ahead error detection remains at chance (\errorAurocOne{}), the shift term's marginal contribution to selective risk stays negligible even after fitting, training costs several times more, and all evidence is from one station.
\end{abstract}
