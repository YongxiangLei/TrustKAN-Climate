\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Problem Formulation}
Let $\mathbf{x}_t\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ denote the observed climate or weather variables at time $t$. Given a look-back window of length $L$, define
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{X}_t=[\mathbf{x}_{t-L+1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_t]\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times d}.
\end{equation}
For a forecasting horizon $H$, the target is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{y}_t=[y_{t+1},\ldots,y_{t+H}]^\top\in\mathbb{R}^{H}.
\end{equation}
The objective is to learn a predictor that produces (i) a point forecast $\hat{\mathbf{y}}_t$, (ii) predictive intervals, and (iii) a reliability score $r_t\in[0,1]$ that can be used for selective forecasting.

\subsection{Temporal KAN Representation}
The input sequence is first transformed by a temporal encoder $g_{\theta}$,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{Z}_t=g_{\theta}(\mathbf{X}_t),
\end{equation}
built from one-dimensional temporal convolutions. Section~\ref{sec:stem} states the stem in full; what matters for the KAN mapping is that $\mathbf{Z}_t\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times m}$ retains one hidden vector per input step. Each hidden vector $\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}$ is then processed by a KAN-style mapping
\begin{equation}
 h_k(\mathbf{z})=b_k(\mathbf{z})+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\phi_{kj}(z_j),
 \label{eq:kanmap}
\end{equation}
where $b_k$ denotes a learnable base mapping and $\phi_{kj}$ is a learnable univariate function. In the dependency-light implementation used in this repository, each $\phi_{kj}$ is represented by Gaussian radial basis functions,
\begin{equation}
\phi_{kj}(z)=\sum_{g=1}^{G}c_{kjg}\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{z-\mu_g}{\sigma_{jg}}\right)^2\right],
\end{equation}
with grid centers $\mu_g$, coefficients $c_{kjg}$, and positive scales $\sigma_{jg}$. This functional decomposition is motivated by the interpretable edge-function view of KANs \cite{liu2025kan}.

\subsection{Full-History Causal Stem}
\label{sec:stem}
A stem of stacked kernel-3 convolutions with symmetric padding has a receptive field that grows by two steps per layer, and at the final position half of each kernel falls on right padding, so two such layers let the last state depend on three input steps whatever $L$ is. A configured look-back is then not an effective one, and the discrepancy is invisible in both the equations and the loss curves. We therefore build the stem so that its reach is a stated property of the construction.

Let $\mathbf{z}^{(0)}=\sigma(\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{e}}\ast_1\mathbf{X}_t)$ be a pointwise embedding of the input to $m$ channels, with $\sigma$ the GELU nonlinearity and $\ast_k$ a convolution of kernel size $k$. For $\ell=1,\ldots,D$ we apply a depthwise kernel-3 convolution of dilation $2^{\ell-1}$, padded on the left only, in a residual branch,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{z}^{(\ell)}=\mathbf{z}^{(\ell-1)}+\sigma\!\left(\mathbf{W}^{(\ell)}\ast_{3}^{\,2^{\ell-1}}\mathbf{z}^{(\ell-1)}\right),
\label{eq:dilated}
\end{equation}
and close with a pointwise mixer, $\mathbf{Z}_t=\sigma(\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{m}}\ast_1\mathbf{z}^{(D)})$. Left-only padding makes every output position a function of its own past exclusively, so the stem is causal by construction and no look-ahead can enter through the boundary. The receptive field of the stack is
\begin{equation}
R(D)=1+\textstyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{D}2\cdot 2^{\ell-1}=2^{D+1}-1,
\label{eq:field}
\end{equation}
so setting $D=\lceil\log_2\!\big((L+1)/2\big)\rceil$ guarantees $R(D)\geq L$. For the $L=365$ used throughout, $D=8$ and $R=511$: the final state can depend on every day in the window. Because the dilated convolutions are depthwise and mixing is confined to two pointwise layers, the stack costs fewer parameters than the two dense convolutions it replaces, so widening the reach does not widen the model. This matters for the ablations: a comparison against a plain KAN of matched budget stays a comparison of inductive bias rather than of capacity.

\subsection{Attention Readout}
\label{sec:readout}
The temporal representation is reduced to an embedding $\mathbf{e}_t\in\mathbb{R}^{m}$ that feeds the forecasting heads and provides the space in which shift is assessed. Taking the final encoder state matches the convention of the recurrent and attention baselines, but it routes a year of context through one $m$-vector, whereas a flattened-lag baseline reads every lag through its own weight. We therefore aggregate over the window as well, with a learned query $\mathbf{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}$,
\begin{equation}
a_{t,s}=\frac{\exp\!\big(\mathbf{z}_{t,s}^{\top}\mathbf{q}/\sqrt{m}\big)}{\sum_{s'}\exp\!\big(\mathbf{z}_{t,s'}^{\top}\mathbf{q}/\sqrt{m}\big)},\qquad
\mathbf{c}_t=\sum_{s=1}^{L}a_{t,s}\,\mathbf{z}_{t,s},
\label{eq:attention}
\end{equation}
and combine the two views through a learned projection,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{e}_t=\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{r}}\left[\mathbf{z}_{t,L};\,\mathbf{c}_t\right].
\label{eq:readout}
\end{equation}
Keeping $\mathbf{z}_{t,L}$ in the concatenation preserves the recency that dominates short-horizon temperature forecasting, while $\mathbf{c}_t$ supplies the seasonal context that dominates at long lead times; Section~\ref{sec:results_ablation} charges each of \eqref{eq:dilated} and \eqref{eq:readout} for its own contribution rather than crediting the pair. The weights $a_{t,s}$ are a distribution over time, so the aggregation is itself inspectable.

\subsection{Joint Point and Quantile Forecasting}
The point head predicts
\begin{equation}
\hat{\mathbf{y}}_t=f_{p}(\mathbf{e}_t),
\end{equation}
while the quantile head predicts $\hat{q}_{t,h}^{(\tau)}$ for quantile levels $\tau\in\mathcal{T}$. For error $u=y-\hat{q}^{(\tau)}$, the pinball loss is
\begin{equation}
\rho_{\tau}(u)=u\left(\tau-\mathbb{I}\{u<0\}\right).
\end{equation}
The training objective is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}=\frac{1}{NH}\sum_{i,h}(y_{i,h}-\hat{y}_{i,h})^2+
\lambda_q\frac{1}{NH|\mathcal{T}|}\sum_{i,h,\tau}\rho_{\tau}\left(y_{i,h}-\hat{q}_{i,h}^{(\tau)}\right).
\label{eq:jointloss}
\end{equation}
The hyperparameter $\lambda_q$ is selected using training/validation data only. Quantile outputs are constrained to be non-crossing in the current implementation by sorting along the quantile dimension.

\subsection{Static Conformal Calibration}
Let $[\hat{\ell}_{i,h},\hat{u}_{i,h}]$ denote the lower and upper model quantiles for a calibration example. Following conformalized quantile ideas \cite{romano2019cqr}, define the nonconformity score
\begin{equation}
s_{i,h}=\max\{\hat{\ell}_{i,h}-y_{i,h},\; y_{i,h}-\hat{u}_{i,h},\;0\}.
\end{equation}
Two calibrations are computed from these scores, because they target different estimands and the results section reports both. The first treats each lead separately, using the $n$ calibration origins as its sample, and gives a radius per lead,
\begin{equation}
\hat{q}^{(h)}_{1-\alpha}=Q_{\lceil(n+1)(1-\alpha)\rceil/n}\left(\{s_{i,h}\}_{i=1}^{n}\right),
\label{eq:marginalradius}
\end{equation}
where $Q$ denotes the empirical quantile using the higher order statistic. This targets marginal coverage at each lead. The second takes each origin's worst lead, $s_i=\max_h s_{i,h}$, and calibrates a single radius $\hat{q}^{\mathrm{sim}}_{1-\alpha}$ on those $n$ values, which targets simultaneous coverage of a whole forecast trajectory. The calibrated intervals are
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{C}_{t,h}=[\hat{\ell}_{t,h}-\hat{q}_{1-\alpha},\;\hat{u}_{t,h}+\hat{q}_{1-\alpha}],
\end{equation}
with $\hat{q}_{1-\alpha}$ taken from \eqref{eq:marginalradius} for the marginal construction and equal to $\hat{q}^{\mathrm{sim}}_{1-\alpha}$ for the simultaneous one. Section~\ref{sec:results_calibration} shows why both are needed: the marginal construction holds its nominal level while the joint coverage it implies does not. Static conformal calibration is treated as a baseline rather than assumed to remain valid under arbitrary temporal shift.

\subsection{Rolling and Adaptive Conformal Calibration}
To respond to non-stationarity, we additionally evaluate sequential calibration. In rolling conformal prediction, the conformal radius at time $t$ is computed from only the most recent $W$ revealed nonconformity scores. The current target is inserted into the score buffer only after the interval for that time step has been issued, preventing label look-ahead.

For Adaptive Conformal Inference (ACI) \cite{gibbs2021adaptive,zaffran2022adaptive}, let $\alpha_t$ denote the effective miscoverage level. After observing whether the issued interval misses the target,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{err}_t=\mathbb{I}\{y_t\notin\mathcal{C}_t\},
\end{equation}
we update
\begin{equation}
\alpha_{t+1}=\Pi_{[\alpha_{\min},\alpha_{\max}]}
\left(\alpha_t+\gamma(\alpha-\mathrm{err}_t)\right),
\label{eq:aci}
\end{equation}
where $\gamma$ is the adaptation rate and $\Pi$ clips the value to a valid range. This creates wider intervals after repeated misses and narrower intervals when empirical errors are below target. Static, rolling, and adaptive variants are compared using coverage, width, interval score, and rolling coverage deviation.

\subsection{Representation-Shift Evidence}
Let $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ and $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ denote the mean and regularized covariance of a bounded subset of \emph{training} embeddings, which is the reference the score measures departure from. For a new embedding $\mathbf{e}_t$, the representation-shift score is
\begin{equation}
d_t=\sqrt{(\mathbf{e}_t-\boldsymbol{\mu})^\top\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\mathbf{e}_t-\boldsymbol{\mu})}.
\label{eq:mahal}
\end{equation}
Rather than interpreting $d_t$ as calibrated probability, it is converted into an empirical reliability component by ranking it relative to the shift scores of the calibration partition, so the reference distribution and the ranking distribution are deliberately distinct. Lower shift implies higher reliability.

\subsection{Reliability Fusion}
The framework uses two default evidence components: an interval-width score and an embedding-shift score. Let $w_t$ denote mean conformal interval width for sample $t$. A width-based reliability score is obtained by mapping $w_t$ relative to the calibration-width distribution, while the representation component is derived from Eq.~\eqref{eq:mahal}. The fused reliability is their weighted geometric mean,
\begin{equation}
r_t=\left(r_{t}^{\mathrm{width}}\right)^{\omega}\left(r_{t}^{\mathrm{shift}}\right)^{1-\omega},\qquad 0\leq\omega\leq1,
\label{eq:fusion}
\end{equation}
which was chosen over an arithmetic combination so that a low value in either component pulls the fused score down rather than being averaged away.

The weight $\omega$ is the part of this construction that decides whether fusion helps or hurts, and fixing it at $\omega=1/2$ in advance is not the conservative choice it appears to be. A component that is uninformative, or whose association with error reverses at long lead times, still carries half the score, and the fused score can then rank forecasts worse than its better input does alone. Freezing an arbitrary constant is therefore weaker discipline than freezing a procedure for choosing it. We freeze the latter: $\omega$ is selected on the calibration partition by minimizing the area under the risk--coverage curve,
\begin{equation}
\hat{\omega}=\arg\min_{\omega\in\Omega}\;\mathrm{AURC}\!\left(\{r_t(\omega)\}_{t\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{cal}}}\right),
\label{eq:weightselect}
\end{equation}
over a uniform grid $\Omega=\{0,\tfrac{1}{G-1},\ldots,1\}$ whose endpoints are the two single-component scores. Two properties follow. The selected fusion is no worse on the calibration split than its best single component, because both components are themselves candidates in $\Omega$, so the mechanism cannot degrade a signal that works. And the data may withhold mass from a component that does not earn it, up to withholding all of it, which turns the fusion from an assumption into a measurement. Only calibration targets and predictions enter \eqref{eq:weightselect}, so no test label informs the choice; the selected $\hat{\omega}$ and the calibration curve behind it are recorded in each run's ledger row. The decision threshold is selected on the same partition. Section~\ref{sec:results_selective} reports what the selection concluded and what it bought.

\subsection{Reliability--Error Validation}
A reliability score is useful only if it is informative about realized error. For each sample, define an aggregate forecast error
\begin{equation}
e_t=\frac{1}{H}\sum_{h=1}^{H}|y_{t,h}-\hat{y}_{t,h}|.
\end{equation}
We evaluate Pearson and Spearman association between $r_t$ and $e_t$, reliability-binned empirical error, and the ability of $1-r_t$ to detect high-error samples. Reliability is expected to be negatively associated with error; failure to establish this relationship is treated as evidence against the proposed reliability mechanism.

\subsection{Selective Forecasting and Risk--Coverage Analysis}
Given threshold $\tau$, the selection indicator is
\begin{equation}
a_t(\tau)=\mathbb{I}\{r_t\geq\tau\}.
\end{equation}
Prediction coverage is
\begin{equation}
C(\tau)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N}a_t(\tau),
\end{equation}
and selective risk under squared loss is
\begin{equation}
R(\tau)=\sqrt{\frac{\sum_t a_t(\tau)\|\mathbf{y}_t-\hat{\mathbf{y}}_t\|_2^2}{H\sum_t a_t(\tau)}}.
\end{equation}
Sweeping $\tau$ yields a risk--coverage curve, consistent with the selective-prediction literature \cite{geifman2017selective,geifman2019selectivenet}. We report the area under this curve (AURC) and retained-set error. A useful reliability mechanism should preferentially abstain on high-error forecasts rather than merely reduce coverage arbitrarily.

\subsection{Interpretability and Explanation Stability}
KAN edge functions are inspected across random seeds, temporal regimes, and controlled perturbations. For two sampled response curves $\mathbf{f}^{(a)}$ and $\mathbf{f}^{(b)}$, stability is quantified using Pearson correlation and normalized distance,
\begin{equation}
D_{\mathrm{norm}}=\frac{\|\mathbf{f}^{(a)}-\mathbf{f}^{(b)}\|_2}{\|\mathbf{f}^{(a)}\|_2+\|\mathbf{f}^{(b)}\|_2+\epsilon}.
\end{equation}
Explanation stability is promoted to a reliability component only if it improves held-out reliability--error association or selective risk beyond interval and shift evidence. Otherwise, it is reported solely as an interpretability diagnostic.
