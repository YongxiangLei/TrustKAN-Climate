\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

Every measured number in this section, in its tables and in its sentences alike, is emitted from the frozen run ledgers by the generators under \texttt{scripts/}; none is transcribed by hand, and a test refuses the manuscript if one is. The only numerals typed directly are the frozen protocol constants that define the evaluation, such as the nominal level and the split fractions. The generator refuses to build a table from a ledger that contains no run matching the current code fingerprint, so a stale aggregation cannot reach the manuscript. Unless stated otherwise, results are the mean over five seeds on the held-out test segment, and all comparisons use the circular moving-block bootstrap over forecast origins described in Section~\ref{sec:experiments}.

\subsection{Deterministic Forecasting}
\label{sec:results_accuracy}

Table~\ref{tab:cet_rmse} reports test RMSE for all ten models. \method{} is mid-pack at one day and deteriorates faster than every learned baseline as lead time grows, ending worst of them at every horizon beyond one day. Its ninety-day error exceeds what persistence achieves at thirty, so the model is beaten across a threefold increase in lead time by copying the last observation; it stays ahead of persistence at a matched horizon, which is the weakest bar the benchmark contains. The transformer is best at all four horizons. The pattern is not seed noise: \method's standard deviation across seeds is at most \benchSeedSdTrust{}\,\degC{}, an order of magnitude smaller than the gaps being discussed, so the model is converging reliably to a worse solution rather than converging unstably.

The classical baselines sharpen this rather than soften it: \classicalAheadLong{} are more accurate than \method{} at thirty and ninety days, fitted on the same windows under the same protocol and carrying none of the machinery this paper proposes. The proposed model is therefore not merely behind the strongest neural comparator at long leads; it is behind the cheapest fitted ones as well.

\generated{tables/cet_rmse.tex}

Table~\ref{tab:cet_paired} reports the pre-specified comparison family. Two comparators were fixed in advance: a budget-matched plain KAN, which isolates the temporal KAN module because it removes that module while holding the parameter budget and every protocol element constant, and the transformer, which was designated the strongest neural baseline. Each of the forty pairs shares a forecast origin set and a seed, so the bootstrap resamples matched errors, and Holm correction is applied across each comparator's twenty-comparison family. Correction does not change any conclusion: every significant pair attains the smallest achievable bootstrap significance level at $5000$ resamples.

\generated{tables/cet_paired.tex}

\method{} wins none of the forty comparisons. Against the plain KAN, the one-day interval includes zero for all five seeds, so the two are indistinguishable there; at 7, 30, and 90 days the plain KAN is significantly better for every seed, with the mean penalty rising monotonically from \pairedKanSeven{} to \pairedKanNinety{}\,\degC{}. Against the transformer, the comparator is significantly better for every seed at every horizon, including at one day, where the mean gap of \pairedTransformerOne{}\,\degC{} is small but consistent enough that no interval covers zero.

The comparison against the plain KAN is the decisive one, because it is the ablation that the temporal KAN module was introduced to pass. Removing that module and spending the same parameters on the simpler mapping improves accuracy at every horizon where the two differ. The monotone growth of the gap with lead time indicates that the module is not merely redundant but is imposing a cost that compounds as the prediction problem becomes harder.

\subsection{Probabilistic Forecasting and Calibration}
\label{sec:results_calibration}

Table~\ref{tab:cet_calibration} reports calibration at a nominal level of \nominalCoverage{}.

\generated{tables/cet_calibration.tex}

Marginal coverage is accurate at all four horizons, deviating from nominal by at most \maxCoverageDeviation{} percentage points. This is the clearest positive result in the study and also the least informative one. Split conformal calibration guarantees marginal coverage under exchangeability of the calibration and test residuals, for any underlying predictor; the same wrapper applied to the persistence baseline would produce the same property. The result therefore confirms that the calibration partition and the conformal implementation are correct, and it says nothing about the KAN representation.

The quantity that a practitioner would rely on behaves quite differently. Joint coverage, the probability that a forecast trajectory is contained in its interval band at every one of its lead times simultaneously, falls from \jointCoverageOne{} at one day to \jointCoverageSeven{}, \jointCoverageThirty{}, and \jointCoverageNinety{} at 7, 30, and 90 days. This is expected behaviour for marginally calibrated intervals under strong serial dependence, and it is why we pre-registered the simultaneous band as well. The simultaneous construction holds up far better, but it still under-covers, reaching \simulCoverageSeven{}, \simulCoverageThirty{}, and \simulCoverageNinety{} at the three longer horizons against a nominal \nominalCoverage{}, and it pays for the improvement with intervals that are \widthRatioSeven{} to \widthRatioNinety{} times wider. A user who reads only the marginal figure would substantially overestimate how often a whole forecast trajectory is trustworthy.

\subsection{Reliability--Error Association and Selective Prediction}
\label{sec:results_selective}

The reliability score fuses normalized conformal interval width with an embedding-shift statistic at frozen equal weights, and drives abstention. Table~\ref{tab:cet_selective} evaluates it against the two components it is built from, which were pre-specified as its ablations.

\generated{tables/cet_selective.tex}

Three results follow, and none supports the proposed score. First, the fused score is beaten by its own width-only component at every horizon, by AURC margins of \fusedPenaltyOne{}, \fusedPenaltySeven{}, \fusedPenaltyThirty{}, and \fusedPenaltyNinety{}\,\degC{} that are large relative to the across-seed standard deviation of \aurcSeedSdLow{}--\aurcSeedSdHigh{}. Adding the shift signal to interval width does not merely fail to help; it degrades a component that works. The shift-only variant is worse than either at all horizons beyond one day, so the frozen equal weighting spends half of the score's mass on the least useful evidence available.

Second, the score does not identify forecasts that will fail. Top-error AUROC is \errorAurocOne{}, \errorAurocSeven{}, \errorAurocThirty{}, and \errorAurocNinety{}, that is, chance to within seed noise, and at one horizon it is below chance. The rank correlation between reliability and realized error is correspondingly negligible, with $|\rho| \le \reliabilityRhoMax{}$. Selective prediction can only work through such a ranking, so this measurement, taken on its own, is sufficient to reject the abstention mechanism.

Figure~\ref{fig:reliability_vs_error} shows where the ranking breaks. Origins are grouped into deciles of each score and the mean absolute error of each decile is plotted, so a working score produces a downward slope. Interval width does: error falls monotonically across its deciles, and the slope steepens with lead time. The embedding-shift term does the opposite at thirty and ninety days, where its most confident decile is its \emph{worst}. Fusing an informative signal with an inverted one at frozen equal weights produces the flat fused curve in the same panels, which is the mechanism behind the AURC ordering above rather than a separate finding.

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{reliability_vs_error.pdf}
\caption{Mean absolute error by decile of each reliability score, averaged over five seeds; the horizontal line is the unconditional error. Scores are rank-transformed so the horizontal axis is comparable across components with different scales. A useful score slopes downward. Interval width does; the embedding-shift term slopes the wrong way at the two longest horizons, and the equal-weight fusion inherits its flatness.}
\label{fig:reliability_vs_error}
\end{figure*}

Third, the downstream consequence is exactly what the ranking predicts. Abstaining on roughly half of all forecast origins reduces RMSE by only \fusedGainOne{}, \fusedGainSeven{}, \fusedGainThirty{}, and \fusedGainNinety{} at the four horizons. Discarding half of the data should remove a substantial share of the error mass if the discarded half were selected informatively; recovering one to three percent confirms that it is not.

The one mechanism that does work is the simplest. Width-only selection improves on no abstention by \widthGainOne{}, \widthGainSeven{}, \widthGainThirty{}, and \widthGainNinety{}, with the benefit growing as forecasts become harder. Conformal interval width, which is available from any quantile model wrapped in split conformal and requires none of the machinery this paper proposes, is a usable selective-forecasting signal on this dataset. That is a real finding, and it belongs to the wrapper rather than to \method{}.

Figure~\ref{fig:risk_coverage} shows the full risk--coverage trade-off behind the single operating point in Table~\ref{tab:cet_selective}, and the separation is not confined to the retention level we pre-registered. Width-only selection lowers retained-set error monotonically as more origins are abstained on, at every horizon. The fused score is nearly flat beyond one day, meaning a practitioner who abstains on its guidance discards forecasts without buying accuracy. The shift-only curve is worse still: at thirty and ninety days it rises as retention falls, so acting on it is worse than not abstaining at all.

\subsection{Ablation Study}
\label{sec:results_ablation}

The pre-registered ablations separate the trained variants, which require their own runs, from the evaluation-side removals, which are recomputed from the A0 artifacts and therefore share its weights exactly. Table~\ref{tab:cet_ablation} reports the eighty trained runs.

\generated{tables/cet_ablation.tex}

A1 is the ablation that matters most and it is the most damaging. Replacing the KAN mapping with a parameter-matched MLP, holding the temporal stem, the budget, and every protocol element fixed, changes RMSE by at most \ablationKanGapMax{}\,\degC{} at any horizon, which is within the across-seed spread. The KAN layer, which supplies the architecture's name and its claim to interpretability, contributes nothing measurable to accuracy. Section~\ref{sec:results_accuracy} showed that the full model loses to a plain KAN; A1 adds that it would also lose nothing by not being a KAN at all. A2 confirms that removing the quantile head leaves point accuracy unchanged, as expected, since the quantile loss is weighted to zero in that variant.

A9 preserves the superseded mean-pooled readout described in Section~\ref{sec:correction} so that the defect stays reproducible. It is worse than A0 by \defectGapOne{}, \defectGapSeven{}, \defectGapThirty{}, and \defectGapNinety{}\,\degC{}, and its across-seed standard deviation at one day is \defectSeedSdOne{}, two orders of magnitude above the corrected model's \correctedSeedSdOne{}. The defect was thus both large and unstable. It also falls behind persistence at \defectBehindCount{} of the \defectMeasuredCount{} horizons, by up to \defectWorstGap{}\,\degC{}, whereas the corrected model stays ahead of persistence everywhere, so the correction is responsible for the difference between a model beaten by copying the last observation and one that is merely uncompetitive.

Table~\ref{tab:cet_eval_ablation} reports the evaluation-side ablations, each expressed as the gain attributable to the component named, so that a positive entry argues for keeping it.

\generated{tables/cet_eval_ablation.tex}

The pattern is consistent with Section~\ref{sec:results_selective} and sharper. Conformal correction (A3) buys real coverage, though the gain shrinks with horizon as the raw quantiles widen. Interval width (A5) is worth keeping relative to the shift term. The embedding-shift component (A4) has a negative gain at every horizon, meaning the score would improve if that component were deleted, and the fusion itself (A6) is worse than its own best single component by the same margins. Abstention (A7) recovers between \abstainGainLow{} and \abstainGainHigh{}\,\degC{} of RMSE, which is the small effect already reported. Applying the rule stated in Section~\ref{sec:experiments}, that a contribution whose ablation shows no incremental value must be withdrawn, the temporal KAN module, the embedding-shift term, the fusion, and the abstention rule are all withdrawn as contributions.

One component survives the ablation cleanly. Adaptive conformal calibration (A8) reduces rolling coverage deviation relative to static split conformal by \adaptiveDevDropOne{}, \adaptiveDevDropSeven{}, \adaptiveDevDropThirty{}, and \adaptiveDevDropNinety{} at the four horizons, and it pays for this with intervals never more than \adaptiveWidthCostThirty{} wider. The trade is favourable at every horizon and improves with lead time, which is where coverage stability is hardest to maintain. Together with width-based selection, this is the second mechanism in the study that works, and like the first it belongs to the conformal wrapper rather than to the proposed architecture or reliability score.

One detail of that metric requires disclosure, since A8 is one of only two surviving positive results. The runner computes rolling coverage on the flattened grid of forecast origins and lead times, so at ninety days a hundred-wide window spans barely more than one origin and measures within-origin dependence as much as drift across the test period. We therefore recomputed the comparison with coverage first averaged over an origin's lead times and then smoothed over a hundred consecutive origins, which makes the horizontal axis time. The verdict is unchanged: adaptive calibration reduces deviation at all four horizons under both definitions, by \adaptiveOriginDropOne{}, \adaptiveOriginDropSeven{}, \adaptiveOriginDropThirty{}, and \adaptiveOriginDropNinety{} under the per-origin definition. Only the magnitudes move, and not in a consistent direction, so we report the pre-registered flattened metric in Table~\ref{tab:cet_eval_ablation} and use the per-origin definition for Figure~\ref{fig:rolling_coverage}, where an interpretable time axis is what the figure is for.

\subsection{Interpretability and Explanation Stability}
\label{sec:results_interpretability}

The interpretability claim has to be assessed on its own terms, because Sections~\ref{sec:results_accuracy} and \ref{sec:results_ablation} have already established that the representation being interpreted is less accurate than a plain KAN and no more accurate than a budget-matched MLP. A model can still be worth interpreting after losing on accuracy, so we ask separately whether the learned curves support the readings the architecture invites.

The benchmark runner stored predictions rather than weights, so the curves are recovered by retraining. Training is deterministic under the frozen protocol, which makes this checkable rather than assumed: each of the \curveRuns{} runs is retrained with its original seed and is only accepted if its test RMSE equals the value in the benchmark ledger. All \curveRuns{} reproduced to the printed precision, which is a reproducibility result in its own right and is what licenses treating these curves as the curves of the models reported above.

Each run yields \curveCount{} univariate maps. Two properties matter. The first is whether the curves are nonlinear at all: the layer is a linear map plus an RBF expansion by construction, so a curve a straight line already explains contributes no structure the linear term did not carry. The RBF expansion accounts for \curveNonlinearShare{} of curve variation on average, but the resulting maps stay close to straight: the median curve has a linear $R^2$ of \curveLinearMedian{}, and \curveNearlyLinear{} of all curves exceed $0.9$. Across \curveCount{} curves, \curveRankMax{} principal components span $95\%$ of the variance in shape. The layer learns a narrow family of gentle monotone bends rather than a rich library of distinct univariate functions, which is consistent with A1's finding that removing it costs nothing.

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{kan_curves.pdf}
\caption{Learned univariate KAN curves at $h=1$. The first four panels show the same curve index in each of the five seeds, chosen among the curves carrying the most variation. The right panel summarizes agreement over all \curveSeedPairs{} seed pairs: index-matched correlation, the best-match bound, and the within-seed control that the best-match bound must be read against. Matched curves agree at chance while the best-match bound is indistinguishable from its control.}
\label{fig:kan_curves}
\end{figure*}

The second property is whether a curve means anything stable across runs, which is what a reader does when told that a model is interpretable. It does not. Averaged over the \curveSeedPairs{} seed pairs at each horizon, the correlation between curve $k$ in one run and curve $k$ in another is \curveMatchedOne{}, \curveMatchedSeven{}, \curveMatchedThirty{}, and \curveMatchedNinety{} at the four horizons, with no pair anywhere in the range \curveMatchedLow{} to \curveMatchedHigh{} departing from zero. Figure~\ref{fig:kan_curves} shows what this looks like: at a fixed index, one seed learns an increasing map, another a decreasing one, another a non-monotone bend. Any statement of the form ``this unit responds to its input in this way'' is a statement about one seed.

Two weaker readings might rescue the claim, and both fail against their own controls. The first notes that matched curves correlate strongly in magnitude, at \curveMatchedAbs{}, with only the sign varying; sign is a symmetry the downstream weights can absorb, so this would suggest correspondence up to a flip. But pairing each curve with an unrelated curve from the other run gives \curveShuffledAbs{}, and the two never differ by more than \curveAbsExcessMax{}. The apparent agreement is a property of the narrow shape space, not of the index. The second reading gives up on indices and asks only whether the same shapes reappear somewhere; the best-match correlation is \curveBestMatchOne{} to \curveBestMatchNinety{}, near perfect. Its control settles it: the same statistic computed within a single run, matching each curve to the most similar \emph{other curve of the same model}, gives \curveControlOne{} to \curveControlNinety{}, differing from the cross-seed value by at most \curveExcessMax{}. With thousands of curves drawn from a three-dimensional space of shapes, a near-identical partner always exists, and finding one across seeds carries no information about reproducibility.

We therefore report the interpretability claim as refuted on CET in the same sense as the others: the curves are individually unstable, their apparent stability is an artifact of low shape diversity, and they are mostly near-linear.

Two further limitations are structural rather than empirical, and the second is the more serious. The KAN layer operates on the output of a convolutional temporal stem, so its inputs are learned latent channels, not observed variables. Even a perfectly stable curve would describe the model's relationship to a coordinate of its own making, and recovering a physical reading would require an additional and separately justified attribution step that this architecture does not provide. Beyond that, Section~\ref{sec:results_receptive} shows that those latent channels are computed from the last \robustFieldTrust{} of the \robustHistory{} available timesteps. Whatever the curves describe, it is a function of three days of weather, not of the year of history the setup implies. This bounds the claim more tightly than the instability does: even had the curves been perfectly reproducible, they would document a mapping over a window the model was never given credit for reading, and stability of a mapping that ignores almost all of its nominal input is not evidence of insight into the system being forecast. We draw no physical conclusions from these curves.

\subsection{Climate Extremes}
\label{sec:results_extremes}

Extreme subsets are defined by the 5th and 95th percentiles of the training targets only, with each origin labelled from its first forecast lead, exactly as fixed in the pre-registered policy. No model is retrained; the stored test predictions are re-scored on the same origin partition, so differences between models are attributable to the models. Table~\ref{tab:cet_extremes} reports RMSE on the union of the cold and warm subsets.

\generated{tables/cet_extremes.tex}

The extreme-event claim fails on the same evidence as the aggregate one. TrustKAN is best at no horizon, and its deficit again widens with lead time, reaching \extremeGapNinety{}\,\degC{} behind the transformer at ninety days. There is no horizon at which the proposed model's behaviour on extremes compensates for its behaviour on average.

Two features of the subsets deserve comment, because they bear on how such tables should be read. First, the partition is dominated by warm origins, \extremeWarmCount{} against \extremeColdLow{}--\extremeColdHigh{} cold ones depending on the horizon, so a single figure for "extremes" is largely a figure for warm extremes; both subsets clear the pre-registered minimum of thirty origins, but the cold subset is small enough that we report it separately rather than relying on the union alone. Cold origins are markedly harder for every model, by roughly one degree at one day relative to ordinary days. This is also why persistence, which is last or nearly last everywhere else, holds the best one-day entry in Table~\ref{tab:cet_extremes}: a subset that selects the calmest days of the year is the one place a copy-forward rule is hard to beat, and its advantage disappears by seven days. Second, at thirty and ninety days most models achieve \emph{lower} error on the extreme subset than on its complement. This is not evidence that extremes are easy: warm extremes in this record fall in summer, when English temperature variance is at its lowest, so the subset selects a low-variance regime as much as an extreme one. The comparison that remains valid is between models on the same subset, which is how Table~\ref{tab:cet_extremes} is constructed.

The same reading exposes where TrustKAN's failure actually lies. At ninety days its extreme-subset RMSE of \extremeTrustNinety{}\,\degC{} is well below its complement error of \extremeComplementNinety{}\,\degC{}, so the degradation reported in Section~\ref{sec:results_accuracy} is concentrated on ordinary days rather than on the rare events the framework was intended to help with.

\subsection{Computational Cost}
\label{sec:results_efficiency}

Cost is recorded for every run in the ledger, and Table~\ref{tab:cet_efficiency} reports it for the models that shared one accelerator. We include it not because efficiency is a contribution but because it closes an alternative explanation for the accuracy ordering: a reader might suspect that \method{} was starved of capacity or compute relative to the baselines that beat it.

\generated{tables/cet_efficiency.tex}

It was not. \method{} sits in the middle of the range on all three measures, and the transformer that beats it at every horizon is the largest and slowest model in the table, which is the direction one would expect if capacity were the binding constraint. The plain KAN that beats it at every horizon beyond one day is larger in parameters but trains in roughly half the wall-clock time, because the temporal recurrence rather than the KAN layers dominates \method's cost. The architecture therefore pays for a component that A1 shows contributes nothing measurable to accuracy. All models are cheap in absolute terms at this problem size, so none of these differences would drive a deployment decision; the relevant conclusion is only that compute does not explain the results in Section~\ref{sec:results_accuracy}.

\subsection{Robustness to Corrupted Inputs}
\label{sec:results_robustness}

The pre-registered corruptions apply to test histories only, so a model is trained once and queried on each perturbed copy. Because the benchmark stored predictions rather than weights, the \robustRuns{} runs behind this section are retrained under the frozen protocol, which raises the question of whether they are the models reported above or merely models trained the same way. The sweep answers it by construction: its first grid entry is the clean history, and a run is refused unless that clean RMSE reproduces the benchmark ledger. All \robustRuns{} were accepted, and the agreement is stronger than the gate requires, since the retrained clean forecasts are identical to the stored benchmark predictions in every element of every run. Identical predictions are not something two differently trained models produce, so the corrupted cells describe the reported models rather than replicas of them. Scope is the pre-specified comparison family of Section~\ref{sec:results_accuracy}. Table~\ref{tab:cet_robustness} reports the \robustLevels{} corruption levels.

\generated{tables/cet_robustness.tex}

The robustness claim fails, and it fails in the same direction as the rest. \method{} is the most fragile of the three models at every corruption and every level: additive noise at the strongest pre-registered scale costs it \robustNoiseTrust{} against \robustNoiseKan{} for the plain KAN, scattered missingness at $40\%$ costs it \robustMissingTrust{} against \robustMissingKan{}, and masking a single recent day costs it \robustBlockOneTrust{} against \robustBlockOneKan{}. No level exists at which the proposed model degrades more gracefully than the comparator it was designed to improve upon.

\subsubsection{Why the Recent Block Matters So Much}
\label{sec:results_receptive}

One column of the table behaves unlike a robustness curve. Extending the masked block from three days to seven does not raise \method's error at all: the two are not merely close but bit-identical in \robustSaturatedTrust{} of the runs, against \robustSaturatedKan{} and \robustSaturatedTransformer{} for the two comparators. Identical errors mean the extra masked days were never read.

Measuring the readout directly confirms the reason. Perturbing one timestep at a time and recording the furthest position that still moves the forecast, \method's readout reaches \robustFieldTrust{} of its \robustHistory{} timesteps, while both comparators reach all \robustHistory{}. Two kernel-3 convolutions and a final-state readout give the architecture a receptive field of three steps, so the year of history it is given arrives as three days. The saturation in the table is the empirical signature of that limit.

Because this measurement now carries weight the corruption sweep alone did not, it is made on the reported models rather than on the architecture in the abstract: each model is retrained under the frozen protocol and is measured only after its test RMSE reproduces the benchmark ledger, at all \robustFieldHorizons{} horizons. The reach does not vary with horizon, and a randomly initialized control of the same architecture gives the same \robustControlTrust{}, \robustControlKan{}, and \robustControlTransformer{} timesteps as the trained models. Agreement between the trained and untrained measurements is what identifies this as a structural property of the design rather than something a particular optimization run happened to produce.

This is the most consequential measurement in the paper, because it is a mechanism rather than another symptom. The accuracy deficit of Section~\ref{sec:results_accuracy} grows monotonically with lead time, which is exactly what a three-day view predicts: at ninety days the comparators can condition on a full seasonal cycle and \method{} cannot. It also reframes the defect of Section~\ref{sec:correction}. The superseded mean-pooled readout reached the whole window but averaged it, destroying recency; the corrected last-state readout preserves recency but reaches almost none of the window. Neither configuration gave the model what its comparators had, which is why correcting the defect produced a competitive one-day forecast and left the long horizons where they were.

It also bounds what the ablation of Section~\ref{sec:results_ablation} established. A1 replaces the KAN mapping inside an encoder whose receptive field is unchanged, so its null result correctly isolates the mapping as contributing nothing. The comparison against the plain KAN does not isolate the mapping in the same way, because that baseline differs in receptive field as well; we therefore attribute the long-horizon gap to the temporal encoder rather than to the presence or absence of a KAN layer. A wider stem or a readout that pools over a longer span is the obvious repair, and it is not one this paper can claim credit for having tested.
