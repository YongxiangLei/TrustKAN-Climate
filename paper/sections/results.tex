\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

Every measured number in this section, in its tables and in its sentences alike, is emitted from the frozen run ledgers by the generators under \texttt{scripts/}; none is transcribed by hand, and a test refuses the manuscript if one is. The only numerals typed directly are the frozen protocol constants that define the evaluation, such as the nominal level and the split fractions. Table captions that state a verdict derive it from the same ledger as the table, so a caption cannot outlive the evidence behind it. The generator refuses to build a table from a ledger that contains no run matching the current code fingerprint, so a stale aggregation cannot reach the manuscript. Unless stated otherwise, results are the mean over five seeds on the held-out test segment, and all comparisons use the circular moving-block bootstrap over forecast origins described in Section~\ref{sec:experiments}.

\subsection{The Effective Receptive Field}
\label{sec:results_receptive}

We report the audit first, because it is the measurement that determines how the rest of the section should be read: a comparison between models with different effective windows is not the comparison it appears to be.

The measurement perturbs one input timestep at a time and records the furthest position back that still changes the forecast. Doing this on a randomly initialized network would answer a question about the architecture; the paper needs the answer for the models it reports, so each model is retrained under the frozen protocol with its original seed and no reach is reported until its test RMSE reproduces the benchmark ledger. That gate is what makes the number a statement about the reported models rather than about replicas of them. A randomly initialized control of the same architecture is measured alongside, which is what distinguishes a structural limit from an accident of one optimization run.

The bottom row of Table~\ref{tab:cet_robustness} reports the result for the swept models, and the audit covers \robustFieldModels{} architectures in total at all \robustFieldHorizons{} horizons. The published temporal stem, two symmetrically padded kernel-3 convolutions with a final-state readout, reaches \robustFieldPublished{} of \robustHistory{} timesteps. The arithmetic is not subtle once stated: symmetric padding places half of each kernel on the right boundary at the final position, so two such layers admit three input steps whatever the configured history is. Nothing in the loss curves, the calibration diagnostics, or the accuracy tables distinguishes that model from one that reads a year. The corrected stem of Eq.~\eqref{eq:dilated} reaches \robustFieldTrust{} steps, and the wide-stem-only variant reaches \robustFieldDilated{}, as Eq.~\eqref{eq:field} requires; the recurrent and attention baselines reach \robustFieldKan{} and \robustFieldTransformer{}. Trained and untrained measurements agree in every case, which identifies the reach as a property of the design rather than of a particular set of weights.

Two features make this diagnostic worth generalizing beyond the architecture that prompted it. It is free: one forward pass per input position on an already-trained model, with no gradient and no retraining. And it is falsifiable in the direction that matters, because it reports a bound on what the forecast can depend on rather than an attribution of what it does depend on. For a reliability audit that asymmetry is the useful one: a model that cannot see an input cannot respond to a change in it, whatever the accuracy tables say.

\subsection{Deterministic Forecasting}
\label{sec:results_accuracy}

Table~\ref{tab:cet_rmse} reports test RMSE for all twelve models.

\generated{tables/cet_rmse.tex}

The corrected architecture attains the lowest mean RMSE at the two longest horizons and sits within a few thousandths of the best model at the two shortest. Bold in that table marks a ranking of point estimates, not a significance statement, and the distinction carries the argument of this subsection: the corrected model is not more accurate than the strongest baseline in any sense the evidence supports. Its across-seed standard deviation is at most \benchSeedSdTrust{}\,\degC{}, comparable to the differences being discussed, which is precisely why the ranking has to be settled by the paired family rather than by the table.

Table~\ref{tab:cet_paired} reports that family. Four comparators were fixed in advance, each isolating one thing. The transformer is the strongest neural baseline. The budget-matched plain KAN removes the temporal KAN module while holding the parameter budget and every protocol element constant. The wide-stem-only variant carries Eq.~\eqref{eq:dilated} without Eq.~\eqref{eq:readout}, so the difference between it and the full model is the attention readout alone. The published architecture is the design this one replaces. Each pair shares a forecast origin set and a seed, so the bootstrap resamples matched errors, and Holm correction is applied across each comparator's twenty-comparison family.

\generated{tables/cet_paired.tex}

Against the transformer the verdict is a tie. The corrected model is favoured in \pairedTransformerWins{} of \pairedTransformerTotal{} pairs, the transformer in \pairedTransformerLosses{}, and \pairedTransformerTied{} are inconclusive after correction; the mean differences are \pairedTransformerOne{}, \pairedTransformerSeven{}, \pairedTransformerThirty{}, and \pairedTransformerNinety{}\,\degC{} in magnitude, an order of magnitude smaller than the seed-matched intervals around them. We therefore claim no accuracy advantage over the strongest baseline, and we note that the favourable mean at the long horizons is exactly the kind of difference that a table of means would let a reader mistake for one.

The other three comparisons resolve. Against the budget-matched plain KAN the corrected model is better in \pairedKanWins{} of \pairedKanTotal{} pairs, at \pairedKanWinHorizons{}, with mean advantages from \pairedKanNinety{} to \pairedKanSeven{}\,\degC{}. This is the comparison the temporal module was introduced to pass and, under a stem that spans its window, the first campaign in which it was a meaningful question at all: when three input steps reached the encoder, what the encoder was could hardly matter. Against the published architecture the corrected model is better in \pairedPublishedWins{} of \pairedPublishedTotal{} pairs, by \pairedPublishedNinety{}\,\degC{} at ninety days.

The comparison against the wide-stem-only variant is the mechanism test, and it behaves as the mechanism predicts rather than as a general improvement would. The attention readout wins \pairedDilatedWins{} of \pairedDilatedTotal{} pairs, all of them at \pairedDilatedWinHorizons{}, and the two models are indistinguishable at the short leads. A readout that supplies seasonal context should matter where seasonal context is what remains predictable and should not matter where recency dominates; the correction is charged for that and for nothing else.

\subsection{Probabilistic Forecasting and Calibration}
\label{sec:results_calibration}

Table~\ref{tab:cet_calibration} reports calibration at a nominal level of \nominalCoverage{}.

\generated{tables/cet_calibration.tex}

Marginal coverage is accurate at all four horizons, deviating from nominal by at most \maxCoverageDeviation{} percentage points. This is the clearest positive result in the table and also the least informative one. Split conformal guarantees marginal coverage under exchangeability of the calibration and test residuals, for any underlying predictor; the same wrapper applied to the persistence baseline would produce the same property. It evidences the calibration partition and the implementation, and says nothing about the representation.

The quantity a practitioner would rely on behaves differently. Joint coverage, the probability that a whole forecast trajectory lies inside its band at every lead simultaneously, falls from \jointCoverageOne{} at one day to \jointCoverageSeven{}, \jointCoverageThirty{}, and \jointCoverageNinety{}. This is expected behaviour for marginally calibrated intervals under strong serial dependence, and it is why the simultaneous construction was pre-registered alongside. The simultaneous band holds up: \simulCoverageSeven{}, \simulCoverageThirty{}, and \simulCoverageNinety{} at the three longer horizons against a nominal \nominalCoverage{}, at a width cost of \widthRatioSeven{} to \widthRatioNinety{} times the marginal band. A user who reads only the marginal figure would substantially overestimate how often an entire trajectory is trustworthy, and the simultaneous row is the one a maintenance decision should be taken against.

\subsection{Reliability--Error Association and Selective Prediction}
\label{sec:results_selective}

The reliability score fuses normalized conformal interval width with an embedding-shift statistic under the weight selected by Eq.~\eqref{eq:weightselect} on the calibration partition, and drives abstention. Table~\ref{tab:cet_selective} evaluates it against the two components it is built from, which were pre-specified as its ablations.

\generated{tables/cet_selective.tex}

What the selection concluded is itself a result. The procedure placed between \fusionWeightLow{} and \fusionWeightHigh{} of the mass on interval width, with a mean of \fusionWeightMean{}, and in \fusionRuns{} runs it selected the equal weighting \fusionEqualRuns{} times. The data were free to choose it and did not. This is the same conclusion an earlier, frozen-weight version of this study reached by refutation --- that the shift term carries much less information than interval width --- but reached here by the method rather than about it, which is the difference between a finding that requires a paper to report and a finding the procedure absorbs.

The score is now informative about realized error, and increasingly so with lead time. Top-error AUROC is \errorAurocOne{}, \errorAurocSeven{}, \errorAurocThirty{}, and \errorAurocNinety{} at the four horizons, and the rank correlation between reliability and realized error strengthens correspondingly to $|\rho|=\reliabilityRhoMax{}$ at ninety days. The boundary condition is the short horizon and it should be stated as such rather than averaged away: at one day the score is at chance. Day-ahead error on this record is dominated by irreducible atmospheric variability, which no internal signal computed from the model's own state can anticipate; a reliability score that claimed otherwise would be suspect. The useful reading is that the score becomes informative exactly where forecasts become hard and where a decision to escalate or abstain has something to act on.

Figure~\ref{fig:reliability_vs_error} shows the ranking directly. Origins are grouped into deciles of each score and the mean absolute error of each decile is plotted, so a working score produces a downward slope.

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{reliability_vs_error.pdf}
\caption{Mean absolute error by decile of each reliability score, averaged over five seeds; the horizontal line is the unconditional error. Scores are rank-transformed so the horizontal axis is comparable across components with different scales. A useful score slopes downward.}
\label{fig:reliability_vs_error}
\end{figure*}

The downstream effect is what the ranking predicts. Abstaining on roughly half of all forecast origins reduces RMSE by \fusedGainOne{}, \fusedGainSeven{}, \fusedGainThirty{}, and \fusedGainNinety{} at the four horizons, with the benefit growing as forecasts become harder. Width-only selection achieves \widthGainOne{}, \widthGainSeven{}, \widthGainThirty{}, and \widthGainNinety{}. The honest summary of the comparison between those two rows is that they are close. Fitting the weight removed the failure in which fusion lost to its own component, which is what the construction guarantees, but it did not buy a substantial improvement over that component: the marginal contribution of the shift term to selective risk remains negligible on this record. The difference from a study that freezes the weight is not that the shift term turned out to be useful. It is that the method reached that conclusion itself, on calibration data, without a separate refutation and without any test label.

Figure~\ref{fig:risk_coverage} shows the full trade-off behind the single operating point in Table~\ref{tab:cet_selective}, and the ordering is not an artifact of the retention level we pre-registered.

\subsection{Ablation Study}
\label{sec:results_ablation}

The pre-registered ablations separate the trained variants, which require their own runs, from the evaluation-side removals, which are recomputed from the A0 artifacts and therefore share its weights exactly. Table~\ref{tab:cet_ablation} reports the trained runs.

\generated{tables/cet_ablation.tex}

A10 and A11 are the two rows that decide whether the architectural story is one change or two. A10 restores the symmetrically padded local stem while keeping the attention readout, and A11 reads the final state alone while keeping the dilated stem; each therefore removes exactly one element. Removing the stem costs up to \ablationStemGapMax{}\,\degC{}, and removing the readout costs up to \ablationReadoutGapMax{}\,\degC{}. Neither element is dispensable and neither accounts for the other, which is what the paired comparison against the wide-stem-only variant showed from the opposite direction. A9 preserves the superseded mean-pooled readout so the earlier behaviour stays reproducible; it is worse than A0 by \defectGapOne{}, \defectGapSeven{}, \defectGapThirty{}, and \defectGapNinety{}\,\degC{}, and its across-seed standard deviation at one day is \defectSeedSdOne{} against the corrected model's \correctedSeedSdOne{}.

A1 answers a question that the published architecture could not pose. Replacing the KAN mapping with a parameter-matched MLP, holding the stem, the readout, the budget, and every protocol element fixed, changes RMSE by at most \ablationKanGapMax{}\,\degC{}. Under the local stem this comparison was uninformative, because three input steps reached the encoder and no encoder could have distinguished itself on them; under a stem that spans the window it is a real test, and the result is what Table~\ref{tab:cet_ablation} reports. A2 confirms that removing the quantile head leaves point accuracy unchanged, as expected, since the quantile loss is weighted to zero in that variant.

Table~\ref{tab:cet_eval_ablation} reports the evaluation-side ablations, each expressed as the gain attributable to the component named, so a positive entry argues for keeping it.

\generated{tables/cet_eval_ablation.tex}

Conformal correction (A3) buys real coverage, though the gain shrinks with horizon as the raw quantiles widen. A4 and A5 charge the two evidence components separately, and A6 charges the fusion against the better of them; the fitted weight is what changed the sign of A6 relative to the frozen weighting, and the magnitude confirms the reading of Section~\ref{sec:results_selective} that the change removes a failure mode rather than adding a capability. Abstention (A7) recovers between \abstainGainLow{} and \abstainGainHigh{}\,\degC{} of RMSE.

Adaptive conformal calibration (A8) reduces rolling coverage deviation relative to static split conformal by \adaptiveDevDropOne{}, \adaptiveDevDropSeven{}, \adaptiveDevDropThirty{}, and \adaptiveDevDropNinety{} at the four horizons, and it pays for this with intervals never more than \adaptiveWidthCostThirty{} wider. The trade is favourable at every horizon and improves with lead time, which is where coverage stability is hardest to maintain.

One detail of that metric requires disclosure. The runner computes rolling coverage on the flattened grid of forecast origins and lead times, so at ninety days a hundred-wide window spans barely more than one origin and measures within-origin dependence as much as drift across the test period. We therefore recomputed the comparison with coverage first averaged over an origin's lead times and then smoothed over a hundred consecutive origins, which makes the horizontal axis time. The verdict is unchanged: adaptive calibration reduces deviation at all four horizons under both definitions, by \adaptiveOriginDropOne{}, \adaptiveOriginDropSeven{}, \adaptiveOriginDropThirty{}, and \adaptiveOriginDropNinety{} under the per-origin definition. Only the magnitudes move, and not in a consistent direction, so we report the pre-registered flattened metric in Table~\ref{tab:cet_eval_ablation} and use the per-origin definition for Figure~\ref{fig:rolling_coverage}, where an interpretable time axis is what the figure is for.

\subsection{Interpretability and Explanation Stability}
\label{sec:results_interpretability}

The interpretability claim is assessed on its own terms, and it does not survive. We report it in full because a reliability paper that suppressed a negative component result would be arguing against its own thesis.

The benchmark runner stored predictions rather than weights, so the curves are recovered by retraining. Training is deterministic under the frozen protocol, which makes this checkable rather than assumed: each of the \curveRuns{} runs is retrained with its original seed and is accepted only if its test RMSE equals the value in the benchmark ledger. All \curveRuns{} reproduced to the printed precision, which is what licenses treating these curves as the curves of the models reported above.

Each run yields \curveCount{} univariate maps. The first question is whether they are nonlinear at all: the layer is a linear map plus an RBF expansion by construction, so a curve that a straight line already explains contributes no structure the linear term did not carry. The RBF expansion accounts for \curveNonlinearShare{} of curve variation on average, but the resulting maps stay close to straight: the median curve has a linear $R^2$ of \curveLinearMedian{}, and \curveNearlyLinear{} of all curves exceed $0.9$. Across \curveCount{} curves, \curveRankMax{} principal components span $95\%$ of the variance in shape.

The second question is whether a curve means anything stable across runs, which is what a reader does when told a model is interpretable. It does not. Averaged over the \curveSeedPairs{} seed pairs at each horizon, the correlation between curve $k$ in one run and curve $k$ in another is \curveMatchedOne{}, \curveMatchedSeven{}, \curveMatchedThirty{}, and \curveMatchedNinety{}, with no pair anywhere in the range \curveMatchedLow{} to \curveMatchedHigh{} departing from zero.

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{kan_curves.pdf}
\caption{Learned univariate KAN curves at $h=1$. The first four panels show the same curve index in each of the five seeds, chosen among the curves carrying the most variation. The right panel summarizes agreement over all \curveSeedPairs{} seed pairs: index-matched correlation, the best-match bound, and the within-seed control that the best-match bound must be read against.}
\label{fig:kan_curves}
\end{figure*}

Two weaker readings might rescue the claim, and both fail against their own controls. The first notes that matched curves correlate strongly in magnitude, at \curveMatchedAbs{}, with only the sign varying, which the downstream weights can absorb. But pairing each curve with an unrelated curve from the other run gives \curveShuffledAbs{}, and the two never differ by more than \curveAbsExcessMax{}: the apparent agreement is a property of the narrow shape space, not of the index. The second reading gives up on indices and asks only whether the same shapes reappear somewhere; the best-match correlation is \curveBestMatchOne{} to \curveBestMatchNinety{}, near perfect. Its control settles it, since the same statistic computed within a single run gives \curveControlOne{} to \curveControlNinety{}, differing by at most \curveExcessMax{}. With thousands of curves drawn from a low-dimensional space of shapes, a near-identical partner always exists.

We therefore report the interpretability claim as refuted on CET, and we note the methodological point that generalizes: stability metrics for learned univariate functions are typically reported without controls, and in a shape space this narrow they are close to uninformative without them. One structural limitation compounds the empirical one. The KAN layer operates on the output of a temporal stem, so its inputs are learned latent channels rather than observed variables, and a physical reading would require a further attribution step this architecture does not provide. Section~\ref{sec:results_receptive} at least removes the sharper objection that applied to the published design, where those channels were computed from \robustFieldPublished{} days; they are now computed from the whole window. That makes the curves worth inspecting in principle, and no more reproducible in practice.

\subsection{Climate Extremes}
\label{sec:results_extremes}

Extreme subsets are defined by the 5th and 95th percentiles of the training targets only, with each origin labelled from its first forecast lead, exactly as fixed in the pre-registered policy. No model is retrained; the stored test predictions are re-scored on the same origin partition, so differences between models are attributable to the models.

\generated{tables/cet_extremes.tex}

Two features of the subsets bear on how such tables should be read. The partition is dominated by warm origins, \extremeWarmCount{} against \extremeColdLow{}--\extremeColdHigh{} cold ones depending on the horizon, so a single figure for extremes is largely a figure for warm extremes; both subsets clear the pre-registered minimum of thirty origins. At thirty and ninety days most models achieve \emph{lower} error on the extreme subset than on its complement, which is not evidence that extremes are easy: warm extremes in this record fall in summer, when English temperature variance is at its lowest, so the subset selects a low-variance regime as much as an extreme one. At ninety days the corrected model's extreme-subset RMSE of \extremeTrustNinety{}\,\degC{} sits below its complement error of \extremeComplementNinety{}\,\degC{} for that reason. The comparison that remains valid is between models on the same subset, and on that comparison the ranking follows the aggregate one; the margin against the transformer at ninety days is \extremeGapNinety{}\,\degC{}, which is well inside the seed spread and is not claimed as an advantage.

\subsection{Robustness to Corrupted Inputs}
\label{sec:results_robustness}

The pre-registered corruptions apply to test histories only, so a model is trained once and queried on each perturbed copy. Because the benchmark stored predictions rather than weights, the \robustRuns{} runs behind this section are retrained under the frozen protocol, which raises the question of whether they are the models reported above or merely models trained the same way. The sweep answers it by construction: its first grid entry is the clean history, and a run is refused unless that clean RMSE reproduces the benchmark ledger. All \robustRuns{} were accepted. Scope is the pre-specified comparison family of Section~\ref{sec:results_accuracy}. Table~\ref{tab:cet_robustness} reports the \robustLevels{} corruption levels over \robustSeeds{} seeds.

\generated{tables/cet_robustness.tex}

Masking recent days remains the most damaging corruption for every model, which is what a forecasting problem dominated by recency should produce. The diagnostic signature that exposed the published defect is gone: under the local stem, extending the masked block from three days to seven left the error not merely similar but bit-identical in a large fraction of runs, because the extra masked days were unreachable. A corruption level that cannot move a metric is a stronger signal than one that moves it the wrong way, and it is what prompted the audit of Section~\ref{sec:results_receptive} in the first place. We record the mechanism here rather than in a limitations paragraph because it is, in our view, the most transferable observation in the paper: saturation of a corruption curve is evidence about architecture, and it is available from a sweep that most robustness protocols already run.

\subsection{Computational Cost}
\label{sec:results_efficiency}

\generated{tables/cet_efficiency.tex}

Cost is recorded for every run in the ledger. The correction is not free, and the table states the price rather than leaving it implicit: a wider effective receptive field and a global readout cost several times the training time of the cheapest neural baseline. At this problem size all models are cheap in absolute terms, so none of these differences would drive a deployment decision, but the ratio is the relevant number for anyone applying the audit's remedy to a larger system.
