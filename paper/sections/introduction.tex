\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Reliability engineering for machine-learned forecasts rests on a small number of measurements: does the model cover its stated interval, does its confidence signal rank realized error, does it degrade gracefully when its inputs degrade. This paper is about two failure modes that none of those measurements can see, and about the diagnostics that make them visible.

The first is a gap between a model's configured context and its effective one. A look-back window is a hyperparameter, so it is reported as if it were a property of the model, but what a forecast can actually depend on is a property of the composition of layers between the input and the readout. Those two quantities can differ by two orders of magnitude without anything looking wrong: the loss converges, the intervals calibrate, the short-horizon accuracy is competitive, and every artifact validates. We encountered this in our own architecture, where a temporal stem of two symmetrically padded kernel-3 convolutions feeding a final-state readout admitted exactly \robustFieldPublished{} of \robustHistory{} configured input steps, trained and untrained alike. Nothing in the equations says so, and no accuracy test can distinguish a model that reads a year from one that reads three days, because both are simply models with some accuracy.

The second is a fusion that damages its own inputs. Combining several evidence signals into one reliability score is standard practice, and the combination weights are usually either tuned on test data, which invalidates them, or frozen in advance, which is treated as the conservative choice. It is not conservative. A frozen equal weighting gives half the mass to a signal that may be uninformative or whose association with error reverses with lead time, and the fused score can then rank forecasts worse than its better input does alone. A reliability score that is beaten by one of its own components is worse than useless, because it is presented as an improvement over that component.

Both failures are cheap to detect once they are named. The receptive-field audit is a loop over single-timestep perturbations on a trained model and requires no training; it returns the furthest input position that can still change the forecast. The fusion repair is to freeze the selection procedure rather than the constant: choose the weight on the calibration partition by minimizing area under the risk--coverage curve, over a grid whose endpoints are the single-component scores. Two properties follow from that construction rather than from any experiment. The selected fusion cannot be worse on calibration than its best single component, because both components are candidates in the grid. And the data may withhold mass from a component that does not earn it, up to withholding all of it, which converts an assumption about how to combine evidence into a measurement of whether to.

We report these as reliability methodology, and we evaluate them on the design that motivated them. \method{} couples a temporal KAN representation with point and quantile prediction, split-conformal calibration on a dedicated partition, an embedding-based shift score, and a fused reliability score driving selective forecasting. The architecture was rebuilt around the audit: the stem is replaced by a causal depthwise stack with doubling dilation whose reach is a closed-form property of its depth, and an attention readout is added so that a year of context need not pass through a single final state. Both changes are opt-in, and the superseded configurations are retained as trained ablations so that the earlier behaviour remains measurable rather than merely described.

The evaluation is pre-registered. Datasets, chronological splits, forecast horizons, seeds, the family of statistical comparisons, and the calibration policy were frozen and hashed before the headline experiments; each claim was written into a claim--evidence matrix against the artifact that would be required to support it; and every run records the checksums of its configuration, code, and data, so a result computed under superseded code cannot survive into a table. The same discipline governs this paper's own history, which includes a refutation of an earlier version of these claims and two defect disclosures. We keep that record intact rather than presenting the corrected system as though it had arrived first.

On accuracy the outcome is parity, and we state it that way throughout. The corrected model is statistically indistinguishable from the strongest baseline: \pairedTransformerWins{} of \pairedTransformerTotal{} seed-matched pairs favour it, \pairedTransformerLosses{} favours the baseline, and \pairedTransformerTied{} are inconclusive after Holm correction. No accuracy advantage over that baseline is claimed anywhere in this paper. What the comparisons do establish is that each corrected element earns its place: the model is significantly better than a budget-matched plain KAN, better than the architecture it replaces at every horizon, and better than a variant carrying the wide stem alone at \pairedDilatedWinHorizons{} and nowhere else, which is where a global readout should matter and nowhere else.

The contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{An effective-receptive-field audit for temporal models.} We define the measurement, apply it to \robustFieldModels{} architectures on trained weights that are required to reproduce their ledger RMSE before any reach is reported, and show it separating a stem that spans its window from one that admits \robustFieldPublished{} steps. The diagnostic catches a model that silently ignores most of its nominal input, which is invisible to accuracy and calibration tests alike, and it costs one perturbation loop.
    \item \textbf{Calibration-selected reliability fusion.} We replace a frozen equal weighting with a frozen selection procedure, which removes the fusion-loses-to-its-component failure mode by construction. On CET the procedure places \fusionWeightLow{}--\fusionWeightHigh{} of the mass on interval width and never selects the equal weighting, in \fusionRuns{} runs; it thereby reaches, from the data, the conclusion that an earlier study of ours had to reach by refutation.
    \item \textbf{Reliability results that now hold.} Error-detection AUROC rises from \errorAurocOne{} to \errorAurocNinety{} across the horizon range and rank correlation with realized error reaches $|\rho|=\reliabilityRhoMax{}$; simultaneous conformal bands hold joint coverage at \simulCoverageThirty{}--\simulCoverageNinety{} against a nominal \nominalCoverage{}; and marginal interval width falls substantially at maintained coverage relative to the published architecture.
    \item \textbf{An architecture whose corrections are separately charged.} A causal dilated stem and an attention readout are introduced together and ablated apart, so neither is credited with the other's contribution, and the paired family isolates the readout to the long leads.
    \item \textbf{A reproducibility apparatus strong enough to have caught its own errors.} Checksum-bound artifacts, a claim--evidence matrix updated against outcomes, and generators that refuse to build a table from runs produced under superseded code. We demonstrate the property that licenses this paper's cross-campaign comparisons: every architecture shared by the two campaigns reproduces its stored test predictions byte for byte under the changed source.
\end{itemize}
